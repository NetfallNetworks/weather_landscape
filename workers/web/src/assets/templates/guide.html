Read the landscape, not the numbers

The house is now, time rolls to the right, and every shape on the canvas is a forecast in disguise. Here is the full key.

one canvas, no labels

What each shape is telling you

There are no axes, legends, or readouts in the landscape — only a little world that rearranges itself to match the next 24 hours. Walk through it shape by shape and it reads like weather, not like a spreadsheet.

The house — you are here
The house sits at the left edge of the canvas and marks the present moment; everything to its right is the future, hour by hour. The smoke rising from its chimney encodes atmospheric pressure — smoke that climbs straight up means high pressure and fair weather ahead, while smoke that sinks and trails low means falling pressure and incoming storms.
The terrain — temperature
Height is heat. The higher the ground rises, the warmer the air at that point in the day, so the rolling profile of the land is really the temperature curve drawn as a horizon. The lower line traces that same temperature across the next 24 hours, so a climbing slope is a warming afternoon and a dip is the cool of night.
The trees — wind strength & direction
A tree's height is the wind's strength: the taller it stands, the harder the wind is blowing. The species of tree tells you which way that wind is coming from.
  • Pine — wind from the North
  • Palm — wind from the South
  • Round / bushy — wind from the East
  • Thin / bare — wind from the West
The clouds — sky cover
Clouds show how much of the sky is covered: a few wisps mean clear, a thick bank means overcast. Each cloud floats above the slice of the timeline when it arrives, so you can see at a glance whether the morning is bright and the evening grey.
Sun & moon — daylight
The sun marks the position of sunrise and the moon marks sunset. The gap between them is the length of the day — a wide span is a long summer afternoon, a narrow one is a short winter day.
The flowers — time anchors
Flowers are mile markers along the timeline. A blue bloom marks midnight and a yellow bloom marks noon, so you can pin any other shape to a real hour just by counting from the nearest flower.
Rain & snow — precipitation
When the forecast calls for wet weather, droplets or flakes fall from the clouds at exactly the spot in the timeline where it is expected — droplets for rain, flakes for snow. No precipitation, no falling pixels, so a dry day simply leaves the air clear.
